Day 26 · Proposal 25 · Part XI · Policy 28

Work and Independence Programme

Helping people move from long-term sickness into suitable, sustainable work through personalised support, retraining, workplace adaptations and meaningful financial incentives.

Employment Independence Productivity Welfare reform
Published for scrutiny This is a citizen-led proposal for public examination and improvement. Detailed eligibility rules, payment levels, costs and delivery arrangements are not presented as settled.

The core idea

Long-term sickness can leave people disconnected from employment even when, with the right treatment, adjustments, training and flexibility, they may want and be able to return to some form of suitable work.

Proposal 25 would create a substantially enhanced support offer for people receiving long-term sickness or disability benefits. The objective is to make a safe return to work financially worthwhile and practically possible—not to punish illness, disability or periods of genuine incapacity.

This sits within the wider economic settlement. More people able to participate on realistic terms can strengthen household incomes, employment, productivity and the public finances. Success must, however, be measured by sustainable outcomes—not by forcing people into unsuitable jobs or simply moving them off support.

The guiding principle Support first. Suitable work must always pay.

Who the programme is designed to help

People who want to return

Individuals who believe work may again be possible but need a practical, supported route back.

People with fluctuating conditions

Support shaped around changing capacity, variable hours and the risk of relapse.

People needing new skills

Free retraining where previous work is no longer suitable or realistic.

People facing practical barriers

Help with adaptations, transport, occupational support and the additional costs of returning.

This programme must never presume that every sick or disabled person can work. People who cannot work must remain protected.

The support package

Delivery should be personalised, coordinated and simple enough to navigate during illness or recovery.

Additional return-to-work payments

Time-limited extra support to make the first stage of employment financially worthwhile.

Temporary tax-free work allowances

A protected allowance during transition so initial earnings create a clear financial gain.

Return-to-work bonuses

A milestone payment when suitable employment is entered and sustained.

Free retraining

Funded qualifications, digital skills and vocational training linked to realistic vacancies.

Occupational support

Specialist advice, phased-return planning and condition-aware employment support.

Workplace adaptations

Practical equipment, accessible technology and changes to duties or the working environment.

Transport support

Help where disability, location or reduced mobility makes the journey to work a barrier.

A named support contact

One accountable adviser coordinating benefits, training, health, employer and workplace support.

Making work genuinely pay

A slogan is not enough. The test should be a person’s real disposable income and stability after tax, benefit changes and the unavoidable costs of working.

A clear financial gain

Returning to suitable work should leave a person materially better off after transport, childcare and disability-related costs.

A gradual transition

Support should taper predictably as earnings and working hours become established, avoiding sudden cliff edges.

Protection if work breaks down

Test a rapid route back to previous support where a medically supported return fails because of health.

Transparent calculations

Give every participant a written better-off assessment before any move into employment.

No one should be asked to risk their health or financial security for a return to work that does not last.

Safeguards and exemptions

The programme must distinguish between inability, uncertainty and unwillingness. Illness and disability are not moral failures. Support must begin with medical evidence, individual circumstances and the risk that work could worsen someone’s health.

No automatic presumption

A diagnosis, benefit award or time away from work must not be treated as proof that somebody is ready for employment.

Health-risk exemption

No participation requirement where work or work preparation would create a substantial risk of deterioration or harm.

Severe or progressive conditions

Clear protection for terminal, progressive, severe and enduring conditions where employment is not realistic.

Treatment and recovery

An exemption or pause during acute treatment, crisis, rehabilitation or a clinically significant recovery period.

Fluctuating capacity

Plans must allow for bad days, relapses, reduced hours and changes in function without immediate penalty.

Independent review

A clear right to challenge decisions, submit specialist evidence and receive support from an advocate.

What employers must contribute

Public funding should not carry the entire burden while workplaces remain inaccessible. Employers participating in the programme should meet clear standards.

Suitable roles

Jobs matched to capability, realistic hours, skills and health needs—not placements designed merely to meet a target.

Flexible and phased returns

Staged hours, predictable scheduling, remote or hybrid work where appropriate, and time for treatment.

Workplace adjustments

Timely equipment, accessible systems and reasonable changes to duties, environments and communication.

Fair pay and progression

No second-class wage, dead-end placement or exploitation of people returning after illness.

The objective is sustained independence and better health—not a short-term reduction in the benefit caseload.

Questions for scrutiny

This proposal requires detailed testing with disabled people, people with long-term health conditions, employers, clinicians, occupational-health specialists, welfare advisers and employment-support organisations.

  • How should “work always pays” be measured after tax, benefit withdrawal and the extra costs of disability and employment?
  • Which payments or allowances would create the strongest incentive without producing unfair cliff edges?
  • How long should transitional protection last, and what happens if a return to work fails because of health?
  • Which conditions and circumstances should create an automatic exemption, and when should individual assessment be used?
  • How should fluctuating and episodic conditions be reflected in hours, attendance and benefit protection?
  • Who should make capability and health-risk decisions, and how can independence and consistency be protected?
  • What duties should employers accept in exchange for public support with recruitment, training and adaptations?
  • How can small employers participate without facing unaffordable costs or complex administration?
  • Which outcomes should be published: job entry, earnings, retention, health, participant experience and employer experience?
  • How should the programme be piloted, evaluated and changed before any wider rollout?
Core commitment Create a support-first Work and Independence Programme that makes suitable work pay, removes practical barriers and protects everyone who cannot safely work.

The proposed direction

People should not be written off when the right support could help them rebuild independence. Equally, nobody should be pushed beyond their health or punished for circumstances they cannot control.

The programme should proceed through co-design, transparent pilots and published evidence, with success measured through sustainable employment, improved incomes, retention, health and participant experience.

Source basis: Manifesto 1.1, Part XI, Policy 28 / Major Promise 25—Work and Independence Programme, read alongside Policy 29 on capability assessment and Policy 30 on hardship protection. Eligibility rules, payment levels, exemptions, delivery arrangements, costs and timetable remain open to scrutiny.
