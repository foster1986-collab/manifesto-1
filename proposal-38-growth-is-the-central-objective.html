PART XVI · POLICY 38

Growth Is the Central Objective.

Link higher living standards and the expansion of the Universal Standard Income to measurable employment, investment, productivity and economic growth.

The central principle

The Universal Standard Income exists for a reason. It is not simply a redistribution mechanism. It is intended to increase economic activity.

Giving households a stronger financial foundation could allow people to reduce debt, build savings, invest, purchase goods, obtain a mortgage, start a business, work additional hours or retrain.

The objective is to convert greater household security into wider economic participation, employment, investment and productivity.

Growth must be measurable before its proceeds are committed.

How households could use additional income

  • Pay down debt: Reduce expensive or persistent household borrowing.
  • Save: Build greater financial resilience and protection against unexpected costs.
  • Invest: Put money into productive or long-term assets.
  • Purchase goods: Support demand across the wider economy.
  • Obtain a mortgage: Strengthen household finances and the ability to secure suitable housing.
  • Start a business: Give people greater freedom to pursue enterprise and self-employment.
  • Work additional hours: Support greater participation where people are able to increase their work.
  • Retrain: Help people acquire new skills and move into more productive employment.

Expected economic channels

The resulting activity should be tested against measurable economic outcomes rather than assumed in advance.

  • VAT receipts: Measure whether additional household spending increases consumption-tax revenue.
  • Income-tax receipts: Assess whether employment, earnings and working participation increase.
  • Corporation-tax receipts: Examine whether stronger activity produces greater taxable business profits.
  • Employment: Track whether more people enter, remain in or progress through work.
  • Business investment: Measure whether firms invest more in people, premises, equipment and expansion.
  • Productivity: Test whether output and economic value rise over time.

The relationship with the USI

The Universal Standard Income is intended to provide greater security while encouraging participation, enterprise and economic activity.

Its long-term expansion must therefore be connected to the economy's demonstrated capacity to support it. Higher future payments cannot be justified solely by forecasts of growth that has not yet occurred.

Employment, investment, productivity, tax receipts and wider economic performance must be measured openly before additional growth-dependent commitments are made.

Growth dividends cannot be spent before they exist.

Fiscal discipline

Forecasts can inform planning, but forecasts are not the same as realised revenue. The manifesto therefore rejects automatically spending anticipated growth in advance.

Any future expansion financed through stronger economic performance should follow evidence that the growth has occurred, is durable and has produced genuine additional fiscal capacity.

  • Publish the economic assumptions behind major spending commitments.
  • Distinguish projected growth from growth already recorded.
  • Measure additional tax receipts rather than counting gross economic activity alone.
  • Account for inflation, borrowing costs and existing spending pressures.
  • Avoid permanent commitments based on temporary or unusually high receipts.
  • Revise the timetable where the evidence does not support the original assumptions.

Measuring whether the approach works

  • Employment, hours worked, earnings and labour-market participation.
  • Household debt, saving, investment and financial resilience.
  • Business creation, survival, expansion and productive investment.
  • Productivity and real economic output per person.
  • Changes in VAT, income-tax and corporation-tax receipts.
  • Inflation, borrowing costs and other effects that could weaken the value of additional growth.
  • Regional and household distribution to establish who benefits from economic expansion.

Questions for scrutiny

  • Which measurements should determine whether economic growth is strong and durable enough to support additional commitments?
  • How should inflation and population change be separated from real improvements in economic output?
  • How should government distinguish revenue created by the policy from revenue caused by wider economic conditions?
  • Over what period should stronger tax receipts be sustained before they are treated as permanent?
  • How would the policy affect household debt, saving, investment and consumer spending?
  • Would additional income increase employment and enterprise, or could it produce different behavioural effects?
  • How should regional inequalities and differences in living costs be considered?
  • What independent economic and fiscal scrutiny should be required?
  • Which outcomes should delay, revise or prevent further expansion of the USI?
  • How should government respond if growth occurs but its benefits are distributed unevenly?

The proposed direction

Policy 38 places economic growth at the centre of Manifesto 1.1. The Universal Standard Income is intended to increase economic activity rather than operate solely as a redistribution mechanism.

Additional household income could support debt repayment, saving, investment, consumption, home ownership, enterprise, work and retraining.

Success would be tested through VAT, income-tax and corporation-tax receipts, employment, business investment and productivity.

The central fiscal safeguard is clear: future growth dividends must not be spent before they have been realised.

Source basis: Manifesto 1.1, Part XVI, Policy 38, “Growth Is the Central Objective”. The source identifies eight potential household uses of additional disposable income and six expected economic channels. It also establishes the fiscal rule that growth dividends cannot be spent before they exist.
