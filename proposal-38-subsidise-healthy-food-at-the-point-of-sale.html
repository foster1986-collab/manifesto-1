DAY 39 · PROPOSAL 38 · PART XXII · POLICY 42

Subsidise Healthy Food at the Point of Sale.

Make the healthier choice visible, practical and cheaper at the checkout.

The core idea

Manifesto 1.1 proposes using additional revenue raised through the food-taxation system to subsidise healthier foods directly at the point of purchase.

Consumers would see the benefit in the price they actually pay. Taxation and subsidy would work together: qualifying less healthy foods could become relatively more expensive while qualifying healthy foods become cheaper.

The source does not specify the subsidy rate, qualifying standards, delivery mechanism or implementation date. These details remain open for modelling and scrutiny.

Use the price system in both directions: discourage poorer choices and reward healthier ones.

Priority foods named in the source

Fruit and vegetables

Fresh fruit and fresh vegetables are named as priority products.

Pulses and grains

Pulses and grains could qualify where the published healthy-food criteria are met.

Nuts and seeds

Nuts and seeds are included among the source’s priority foods.

Poultry

Poultry is also listed, with the detailed eligibility rules still to be defined.

Other qualifying foods

Other products could qualify where they meet transparent, evidence-based healthy-food criteria. The final definition remains open for modelling and scrutiny.

How the subsidy could work

The defining commitment is that consumers should receive the benefit at the point of sale. The operational model must be designed around that outcome rather than leaving support hidden elsewhere in the supply chain.

  • Qualify: Establish clear healthy-food criteria and identify eligible products through a transparent national process.
  • Reduce: Apply the subsidy so the qualifying product’s checkout price is lower than it would otherwise be.
  • Reimburse: Create a workable route for retailers or suppliers to reclaim the approved subsidy accurately and promptly.
  • Show: Make the saving visible to consumers so the policy is understandable and its benefit can be verified.
The source sets the objective. It does not fix the subsidy rate, eligibility test or delivery system.

Design choices government must resolve

  • Eligibility: Define the nutrition, processing and product criteria that qualify for support.
  • Subsidy level: Model a discount large enough to influence affordability without wasting public money.
  • Delivery route: Choose between automatic till discounts, retailer reimbursement or another auditable model.
  • Retail coverage: Ensure supermarkets, convenience stores, markets and online retailers can participate fairly.
  • Geography: Address rural, island and deprived areas where availability and prices may differ substantially.
  • Review: Publish results and adjust the scheme if prices, access or nutritional outcomes do not improve.

Protecting value, access and public trust

A point-of-sale subsidy could improve affordability, but weak design could allow the benefit to be absorbed into margins, miss smaller retailers, reward products of uncertain nutritional value or create a costly administrative system.

Safeguards must therefore be built into the final model.

  • Pass-through: Verify that public support reduces the consumer’s actual price rather than increasing margins elsewhere.
  • Low-income households: Test whether savings reach households facing the greatest affordability pressures.
  • Small retailers: Keep participation practical for independent shops and avoid concentrating benefits in large chains.
  • Availability: Pair lower prices with attention to local supply, product quality and healthy-food access.
  • Fraud and error: Use auditable product lists, transactions and claims while keeping administration proportionate.
  • Funding stability: Model revenue volatility and define what happens if food-tax receipts do not cover the scheme.

Measuring whether it works

  • Changes in checkout prices for qualifying foods, including differences between regions and retailers.
  • Changes in purchasing, diet quality and substitution between subsidised and non-subsidised products.
  • Reach among lower-income households and communities with poor access to affordable healthy food.
  • Retailer participation, reimbursement speed and administrative costs for businesses and government.
  • Evidence of price inflation, margin capture, fraud, misclassification or other unintended effects.
  • Public-health outcomes over time, while recognising that food policy is only one influence on health.
Make healthier food cheaper at the moment people decide what to buy.

Questions for scrutiny

This proposal should be tested with nutrition scientists, public-health specialists, economists, consumer and poverty groups, people with diet-related ill health, farmers, producers, supermarkets, independent retailers, wholesalers, tax specialists, local government and the devolved administrations.

  • Which foods should qualify, and how should the healthy-food criteria be defined and updated?
  • Should eligibility depend on nutrition, processing, product category or a combination of factors?
  • What subsidy level would materially improve affordability and influence purchasing decisions?
  • Should the discount be universal, targeted by household need or varied by product and location?
  • How can the full benefit be guaranteed to reach consumers rather than being absorbed into margins?
  • What delivery model would work reliably at tills, online checkouts, markets and smaller retailers?
  • How should retailers be reimbursed, audited and protected from unreasonable cash-flow burdens?
  • How can rural, island and deprived communities benefit where healthy-food availability is limited?
  • How should seasonal prices, imports, farming impacts and domestic food production be treated?
  • What happens if food-tax revenue is lower or more volatile than the cost of the subsidy programme?
  • How should fraud, misclassification, gaming and disproportionate administrative costs be prevented?
  • Which price, purchasing, equality and health outcomes should trigger revision or withdrawal?

The proposed direction

Proposal 38 would use additional revenue from the food-taxation system to reduce the point-of-sale price of qualifying healthier foods.

Priority products named in the source are fresh fruit, fresh vegetables, pulses, grains, nuts, seeds and poultry, alongside other foods meeting defined criteria.

Before implementation, government must publish the eligibility rules, subsidy levels, delivery and reimbursement model, retailer requirements, geographic and equality assessment, funding plan, anti-fraud controls and review criteria.

Those details are essential to determining whether the proposal is fair, workable and effective.

Tax unhealthy food more heavily while making healthy food cheaper.

Source basis: Manifesto 1.1, Part XXII, Policy 42, “Subsidise Healthy Food at the Point of Sale”, described as Major Promise 37: Make Healthy Food Cheaper. The source proposes using additional food-tax revenue to subsidise priority healthy foods directly at purchase. It does not specify qualifying standards, subsidy rates, the delivery or reimbursement mechanism, administrative costs, funding treatment during revenue shortfalls or an implementation date; these remain open for modelling and scrutiny.
