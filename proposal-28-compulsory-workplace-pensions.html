Day 29 · Proposal 28 · Part XII · Policy 31

Compulsory Workplace Pensions

Every job. Every worker. A pension that follows you.

Pensions Work Security Long-term reform

The core idea

Retirement saving as part of every job

Manifesto 1.1 proposes that every person in employment should build a workplace pension. The model would replace today’s opt-out structure with compulsory participation, so that every job includes retirement saving from the beginning.

Each employee would make a statutory contribution. The employer would match it, and government would provide tax relief equal to 25% of the employee contribution. The required contribution rate would rise gradually rather than being imposed at its final level immediately.

The aim is to create funded retirement provision throughout working life, reduce long-term dependence on the State Pension and support Policy 32’s proposed minimum retirement-income safety net.

The core commitment

Every person in employment builds a pension—with an employer match and government support.

Shared responsibility

The three-way contribution

Employee

A statutory contribution deducted through payroll and invested for retirement.

Employer

A matching contribution, making retirement saving part of the cost and reward of every job.

Government

Tax relief equal to 25% of the employee contribution, subject to a clear and consistent legal design.

Portable pension

A pension record that follows the individual across jobs, sectors and changes in working pattern.

System reform

From automatic enrolment to universal participation

The current UK system requires employers to provide a workplace pension and automatically enrol many workers. However, eligibility thresholds and exemptions apply, and an enrolled worker can normally opt out.

Proposal 28 would go further: every person in employment would participate, with no routine opt-out from the statutory minimum.

Current model

Automatic enrolment

Automatic enrolment generally applies from age 22 to State Pension age where earnings reach the statutory threshold.

In most current automatic-enrolment schemes, the legal minimum is 8% in total, including at least 3% from the employer.

Proposed model

Universal participation

Every person in employment would have a workplace pension and make at least the statutory minimum contribution.

The employer contribution would match the employee contribution rather than merely satisfy a lower separate minimum.

How it would work

Building a pension through employment

  1. Employment begins The employer registers the worker and pension arrangement from the first eligible pay period.
  2. Employee contribution A statutory share of pensionable pay is deducted through payroll.
  3. Employer match The employer contributes an equal amount under the proposed matching rule.
  4. Government relief Government adds support equal to 25% of the employee contribution.
  5. One continuing record The worker retains a clear, portable pension record when changing jobs.

Illustrative only: employee pays £40 + employer pays £40 + government adds £10 = £90 invested.

Protection and implementation

Safeguards for a compulsory system

Compulsion creates responsibilities for government, employers, pension providers and regulators. A statutory minimum must be affordable, transparent and protected from excessive charges, poor administration and unsuitable investment.

Low and variable pay

Phase contribution rates carefully and test their effect on take-home pay, debt and household hardship.

Multiple jobs

Combine contributions clearly so people with several small jobs do not lose employer support or create fragmented pots.

Changing employers

Use a portable default arrangement or effective consolidation so workers can follow and understand their pension.

Parental and sick leave

Define how statutory pay, career breaks and temporary absences affect contributions and employer matching.

Investment protection

Apply regulated default funds, charge controls, governance duties and clear routes for complaints and compensation.

Plain-language records

Show employee, employer and government additions, fees, investment performance and projected retirement income.

Small employers

Phase implementation, simplify payroll and consider transitional support without weakening the employee entitlement.

Non-standard work

Resolve coverage for agency work, zero-hours contracts, short contracts, platform work and employment-status disputes.

The wider settlement

Relationship with Policy 32

Policy 31 is intended to build private pension income. Policy 32 would provide a proposed minimum retirement income of £18,000 a year, with the state contribution reduced as private pension income rises.

The two policies must therefore be modelled together. The design must avoid creating a weak incentive to save, penalising modest pension pots or promising state savings before contribution rates, investment returns, retirement ages and transition costs are known.

The aim is more funded retirement income—not a guarantee that private saving can quickly replace the State Pension.

Public scrutiny

Questions that must be answered

The policy should be developed with workers, employers, trade unions, pension providers, actuaries, consumer groups, the self-employed, payroll specialists, tax experts and organisations representing people on low and unstable incomes.

  1. What statutory employee contribution should apply at launch, and what final rate should the system target?
  2. Should employer matching apply from the first pound earned, or should any lower earnings threshold remain?
  3. How should compulsory deductions be made affordable for workers facing low pay, debt or temporary hardship?
  4. Does 25% government tax relief mean 25 pence for every £1 paid by the employee, and how should it interact with marginal tax rates?
  5. Should the same minimum apply to employees, workers, agency staff and people in platform or gig-economy arrangements?
  6. How should self-employed people be included where there is no employer available to provide a matching contribution?
  7. Should pension pots follow the worker automatically, be consolidated into one default account or remain with separate schemes?
  8. What limits should apply to fees, investment risk, default funds and access before retirement?
  9. How should employers, particularly small firms, absorb the cost of a full contribution match?
  10. Would Policy 32’s means test reduce the incentive to save, and how should private pension income be treated fairly?
  11. What are the fiscal costs of tax relief and transitional employer support, and when might State Pension spending fall?
  12. What independent modelling, pilot evidence and parliamentary scrutiny should be required before compulsion begins?

The proposed direction

Compulsory saving—with the final design open to scrutiny

Proposal 28 would turn workplace pension saving from an opt-out system into a compulsory employment standard. The contribution rate would rise gradually, with affordability tests and implementation support developed before rollout.

The precise percentage, earnings basis, age coverage, treatment of self-employment, portability mechanism and transition timetable are not settled.

This citizen-led proposal requires actuarial modelling, distributional analysis, legal review and consultation before national implementation.

Core commitment

Every job will build a pension. Employees contribute, employers match and government supports saving.
