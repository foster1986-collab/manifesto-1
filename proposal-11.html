A Pragmatic Net Zero Policy

Slow and Restructure Net Zero. Keep Net Zero as a long-term ambition, while taking a slower, pragmatic and technology-neutral approach to the transition.

The principle

The route to decarbonisation matters as well as the destination. Manifesto 1.1 proposes a transition that takes account of what households can afford, the security of energy supplies and the readiness of the technologies needed to deliver it.

1. Five considerations at the heart of the policy

  • Energy prices. The cost of energy must be considered alongside the ambition to decarbonise.
  • Energy security. The transition must take account of the security of Britain's energy supplies.
  • Industrial competitiveness. The implications for British industry form part of the policy judgement.
  • Technological readiness. The pace of change must take account of the technologies available to support it.
  • Household affordability. What households can afford is an explicit consideration, not an afterthought.

These are the five considerations named in the master manifesto. The proposal sets a direction for policy; it does not yet provide a detailed delivery timetable or a quantified assessment of its effects.

Major Promise 12 in the master

2. The policy commitment

The ambition remains. The approach changes.

The master sets out the commitment as follows:

Net Zero remains a long-term ambition. But we reject the idea that Britain should pursue decarbonisation regardless of:

  • energy prices;
  • energy security;
  • industrial competitiveness;
  • technological readiness;
  • or household affordability.

The transition will therefore be slower, pragmatic and technology-neutral.

3. How the approach changes

Retain the long-term ambition

The commitment does not abandon Net Zero as an ambition. It changes the proposed approach to pursuing it, placing the five stated considerations alongside decarbonisation.

Slow and restructure the transition

The master calls for a slower, pragmatic transition. It does not specify a replacement target year, interim milestones or which individual measures would change. Those details remain to be developed and scrutinised.

Take a technology-neutral approach

Technology-neutrality is an explicit part of the commitment. This section does not set a prescribed technology mix or a formula for choosing between technologies; technological readiness is one of its stated considerations.

What this proposal does not claim

No specific reduction in bills, public spending saving or emissions outcome is quantified in this section of the master. This release does not present any such outcome as guaranteed.

4. How this fits the energy programme

This is the opening proposal in Part V — Energy. The master then sets out four separate commitments, each with its own scope.

  • Nuclear power. Redirect a major proportion of decarbonisation-related energy levies towards nuclear generation, research, grid resilience and fuel security.
  • North Sea oil and gas. Continue development where economically and environmentally appropriate while replacement technologies develop.
  • A social energy tariff. Introduce a national tariff for low-income households, with eligibility based on income and household circumstances.
  • Standing charges. Abolish domestic gas and electricity standing charges, recovering legitimate costs through unit prices with regulatory scrutiny.

These related commitments will be explored in separate releases.

5. Questions for public scrutiny

What timetable and interim milestones would make this approach credible, and how should its emissions consequences be assessed?

How should affordability, energy security and industrial competitiveness be weighed alongside the costs and risks of a slower transition?

What evidence should establish technological readiness, and how should progress and policy changes be reported publicly?
