A Four-Day Working Economy

A flexible full-time model designed to work alongside a seven-day economy.

32 HOURS · FOUR DAYS

The core idea

Britain has a productivity problem. Weak productivity and economic output constrain wages, investment, public finances and the country’s capacity to grow.

Proposal 22 would promote a four-day working week as part of a wider shift towards a flexible, seven-day economy. The purpose is not to close Britain for three days. It is to organise working time more effectively while services and businesses can operate across the whole week.

The intended standard full-time pattern would increasingly move towards approximately 32 hours across four days, where economically and operationally appropriate.

32 HOURS across four working days, where appropriate
Four days for the worker does not mean four days for Britain.

One policy, two connected levels

The individual working pattern

A standard full-time week increasingly organised around approximately four days and 32 hours, where appropriate.

The national operating week

Public services and businesses encouraged to operate across seven days through staggered and flexible staffing.

The intended outcome

Better organisation of working time, stronger productivity and increased economic output.

The wider mission

More jobs, wider participation and a modern economy capable of operating throughout the week.

Designed alongside Policy 26

Policy 25 must be read in context with Policy 26, Britain: Open Seven Days a Week. The two proposals are intended to operate together.

A shorter standard full-time pattern would sit within a longer national operating week. Different teams and workers could cover different days, allowing organisations to remain available seven days a week without requiring every individual to work seven days.

Policy 25

Moves the standard full-time pattern towards approximately 32 hours across four days where appropriate.

Policy 26

Moves public services and the wider economy towards increasing seven-day operation.

Staggered coverage

Different working patterns can provide continuity across the week while preserving individual rest time.

Flexible participation

A wider range of schedules may help more people find working patterns suited to their circumstances.

A four-day working week and a seven-day economy are not opposites. They are two parts of the same flexible model.

What the model is intended to unlock

Higher productivity

Encourage organisations to focus on output, organisation and the effective use of working time.

Greater economic output

Use a seven-day operating model to expand activity across more of the week.

More employment

Longer operating coverage may require additional teams, roles and shift combinations.

Additional work

People who want further or extra employment could have more opportunities to take it up.

Better access

Extended operating days could make public and private services easier to reach.

A 24/7 economy

Align work, services, business activity and later weekend-banking reform within a modern operating model.

Productivity, flexibility and safeguards

The objective is not simply to compress the same pressures into fewer days. The policy aims to encourage better organisation, investment, technology, management and working practices so stronger output can be achieved within a more focused standard pattern.

The four-day model is one part of a wider productivity strategy. Reducing scheduled hours alone does not guarantee stronger economic growth.

Sector differences

The model may work differently across offices, manufacturing, retail, hospitality, healthcare, transport and emergency services.

Operational need

Implementation must recognise services requiring continuous coverage or unable to reduce hours immediately.

Small employers

Transition arrangements must consider staffing costs, recruitment pressures and administrative capacity.

Worker protection

Flexible scheduling must not become unpredictable hours, involuntary overtime or weakened rest protections.

Pay and contracts

Salaries, hourly pay, overtime and contractual hours require clear policy design.

Evidence and review

Phasing should be measured against productivity, wellbeing, vacancies, service access and sector-specific results.

The rota question

The master manifesto includes illustrative Monday-Friday, Tuesday-Saturday and Wednesday-Sunday working spans. Each example covers five days, so its relationship to a literal four-day, 32-hour model requires clarification before any rota is specified.

The settled principle is staggered and flexible coverage across a seven-day economy. Exact rota patterns remain an implementation question for scrutiny rather than a fixed commitment in this release.

The test is whether Britain becomes more productive, flexible and economically active.

Questions for scrutiny

  • How should the move towards approximately 32 hours across four days be phased?
  • Should pay be maintained, adjusted or determined through sector and workplace agreements?
  • Which sectors are suitable for early adoption?
  • How should smaller employers be supported with transition costs?
  • What evidence should determine whether productivity and total economic output have increased?
  • How can flexible scheduling protect predictable hours, rest, family life and worker choice?
  • How should the model interact with overtime, second jobs, benefits, pensions and employment rights?
  • How should Policies 25 and 26 be piloted and evaluated together?

Core commitment

Government will promote a move towards an approximately 32-hour, four-day standard full-time pattern where economically and operationally appropriate. The model will be developed alongside Policy 26’s seven-day economy so shorter individual working patterns can coexist with wider national operating hours.
