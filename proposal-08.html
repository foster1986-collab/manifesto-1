Proposal 08

Universal Healthcare Through Compulsory Insurance

Can Britain preserve universal healthcare while replacing an increasingly strained NHS funding and delivery model with compulsory insurance, regulated competition and government assistance?

The NHS holds a unique place in British life. It is part of our national story, and the principle behind it — that everybody should have access to healthcare when they need it — must be protected.

But protecting that principle does not mean pretending that the current model is working as well as it should.

Britain will retain universal healthcare. But the way we finance and deliver it must change.

Proposal 08 would create a system of Universal Healthcare Insurance.

Every resident would be required to hold qualifying health insurance, while government would provide financial assistance to people who cannot reasonably afford the premiums.

1. The principle

Universal entitlement + compulsory insurance + government assistance.

Nobody would be denied medically necessary healthcare because they could not afford insurance.

The system would remain universal — but the model would be fundamentally different from the NHS structure Britain has today.

Measure Proposed approach
Healthcare entitlement Universal access to medically necessary healthcare
Insurance Compulsory qualifying health insurance for every resident
Low-income support Government premium subsidies and top-ups
Pre-existing conditions Protected access to essential healthcare and qualifying cover
Provider model Regulated competition between qualifying healthcare providers
Core objective More capacity, higher standards, faster access and long-term sustainability

2. Why reform the current system?

Britain can value the NHS deeply while still asking whether its current structure is sustainable for the decades ahead.

The healthcare system was created for a very different demographic and medical landscape.

Britain now has:

  • an ageing population;
  • more people living longer with chronic and complex conditions;
  • increasing demand for diagnostics and treatment;
  • significant workforce pressures;
  • long waiting lists;
  • capacity shortages; and
  • patients being treated in corridors and unsuitable spaces.
Respecting the NHS does not mean refusing to reform it.

If Britain wants universal healthcare to survive, its financing and delivery model must be capable of meeting modern demand.

3. Compulsory insurance

Under Proposal 08, every resident would be required to hold qualifying health insurance.

Government would establish a statutory minimum package so that qualifying insurance could not be reduced to inadequate or meaningless cover.

The system would require clear national standards governing:

  • minimum healthcare coverage;
  • access to medically necessary treatment;
  • consumer protection;
  • provider quality;
  • treatment of pre-existing conditions;
  • premium subsidies; and
  • protection for vulnerable and high-need patients.

4. Nobody left behind

Compulsory insurance can only form part of a universal healthcare system if inability to pay never becomes inability to receive treatment.

Nobody should lose access to medically necessary healthcare because they are poor, elderly, disabled, chronically ill or expensive to treat.

Government support would protect:

  • low-income households;
  • children;
  • vulnerable people;
  • people with significant healthcare needs;
  • people with pre-existing conditions;
  • people with long-term illnesses; and
  • people facing catastrophic healthcare costs.

Premium subsidies and top-ups would ensure that compulsory insurance did not become a financial barrier to treatment.

5. Competition and patient choice

One of the central arguments behind the proposal is that greater provider competition could help improve standards and reduce waiting times.

Patients should not simply be expected to tolerate unacceptable service because they have nowhere else to go.

If a provider repeatedly delivers poor service or excessive waiting times, patients should have the ability to choose another qualifying provider.

That creates an incentive for providers to compete on:

  • quality;
  • waiting times;
  • patient experience;
  • clinical outcomes;
  • innovation; and
  • capacity.

This would be a regulated healthcare market, not an unregulated free-for-all.

6. Building more healthcare infrastructure

Britain's healthcare challenge is not only about money. It is also about physical capacity.

The country needs more:

  • hospitals;
  • diagnostic centres;
  • clinics;
  • community facilities;
  • treatment capacity;
  • technology; and
  • trained healthcare professionals.

A competitive insurance-funded model is intended to make unmet demand visible and create stronger incentives for providers to invest in additional capacity.

Where patients need more services, existing providers could expand and new providers could enter the market — subject to safety, workforce and quality requirements.

Choice means little if every provider has the same shortage of beds, staff and appointments. Reform must create genuinely additional capacity.

7. What happens to the NHS?

This proposal is not about switching off existing NHS hospitals or abandoning the enormous public healthcare infrastructure Britain already owns.

It would involve a transition from the current model towards a universal insurance-based system with a mixed provider landscape.

  • existing NHS hospitals and services would form part of the transition;
  • public providers could continue operating within the new system;
  • national healthcare standards would remain matters of public policy;
  • government would remain responsible for ensuring universal access; and
  • the future ownership and payment structure of existing NHS organisations would require detailed design.

The word privatisation can sometimes obscure the real policy questions.

The important questions are:

  • Who pays?
  • Who provides?
  • Who regulates?
  • What are patients guaranteed?
  • What happens when somebody cannot pay?

Proposal 08 answers the principle clearly: universal healthcare remains.

8. Funding and government subsidies

Manifesto 1.0 currently contains an indicative mature annual programme envelope of approximately:

£60–£90 billion for healthcare subsidies.

This is a working estimate, not a finished costing.

The true public cost would depend on:

  • insurance premium levels;
  • the minimum statutory healthcare package;
  • income thresholds for government assistance;
  • treatment of high-risk patients;
  • the cost of regulation;
  • the treatment of existing NHS expenditure; and
  • transition costs.

An important admission

This proposal is not implementation-ready.

Healthcare reform on this scale cannot responsibly be designed from a headline.

Premiums, subsidies, provider payments, workforce effects, regulation, transition costs and the treatment of existing NHS assets all require detailed modelling.

This proposal should therefore be subjected to independent health-economic scrutiny before any attempt at implementation.

9. The difficult questions

  • What would a standard qualifying insurance policy cost?
  • How much government support would low-income households receive?
  • How should premiums vary by income, age or circumstance?
  • How do we guarantee affordable cover for people with pre-existing conditions?
  • How do we prevent insurers from selecting only healthier patients?
  • What must every qualifying policy cover?
  • Should excesses or co-payments exist?
  • How would existing NHS hospitals operate within the new system?
  • How do we expand capacity without simply moving the same doctors and nurses between organisations?
  • Would insurance administration create unnecessary bureaucracy?
  • Would genuine competition reduce waiting lists?
  • Would the model create more healthcare infrastructure?
  • How do we prevent the emergence of an unacceptable two-tier system?

10. A proposal to test — not defend at all costs

This is one of the most consequential and controversial proposals in Manifesto 1.0.

It should therefore face a higher evidential standard, not a lower one.

The next stage should include:

  • independent health-economic modelling;
  • distributional analysis of what different households would pay;
  • comparison with successful and unsuccessful international insurance models;
  • workforce and infrastructure modelling;
  • transition planning for existing NHS assets and services; and
  • scrutiny from patients, clinicians, NHS staff, economists, insurers and health-policy specialists.
A national treasure should be strong enough to reform.

The NHS's place in Britain's history deserves respect.

But respect cannot mean accepting corridor care, excessive waiting lists or insufficient capacity as inevitable.

If evidence demonstrates that another universal model could deliver faster care, higher standards, greater capacity and stronger long-term sustainability while protecting everybody regardless of income or medical history, Britain should be willing to examine it.

If the proposed model cannot meet those tests, change it.

11. Proposal 08 — the question

Can Britain preserve universal healthcare while replacing an increasingly strained funding and delivery model with compulsory insurance, regulated competition and government assistance — without leaving anybody behind?

This proposal will be controversial.

That is precisely why it needs serious scrutiny.

Let's test it.
